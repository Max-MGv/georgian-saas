Four ways to make the demo sell

The demo is live and technically works — a real tenant, real booking engine, real back office. But a cold visitor lands on what looks like a small winery's website in Kakheti, with no signal that it's a product, and the back office they're meant to be impressed by is completely empty. Below: what's actually on screen today, then four directions, drawn on top of the real UI.

1

The nag banner says “unfinished”

“Finish setting up your account” sits on top of every admin page. To a prospect evaluating whether this product is real, it reads as a half-built tool — the opposite of the intended message.

2

Empty state where the value lives

No orders found is the punchline of the whole demo journey. Industry data is unambiguous here: generic or hollow demos convert around 18%, while demos with believable, situation-specific data run 25%+, and interactive ones the prospect drives reach ~38%.

3

No frame, no product story

Nothing on the page explains what Vineworks is, who it's for, or what it costs. The depth you've actually built — masterclasses, per-company pricing, packing sheets, Georgian/English, card payments — is invisible unless you already know to look for it.

4

A dead end behind the admin link

Opening /admin directly (a shared link, a reload, an expired session) lands on a bare email + password form with no credentials and no way back to the demo. That visitor is simply gone.

Directions

Four ways to structure it

These aren't visual variants of the same idea — they're four different answers to “how does a stranger understand this in ninety seconds?”. They combine well; the sequencing at the end says which to build first.

01

An interstitial that frames the product and lets the visitor pick what they came to see, before dropping them into the live site.

Sits at the root. Each card deep-links into the real product with the relevant tour armed.

Why it works

It answers the three questions a stranger has — what is this, is it real, and what's in it for me — in one screen, then gets out of the way. This is the standard “demo hub” shape used by Navattic and Storylane customers, and the reason it exists is that self-directed demos get engagement from roughly a quarter of visitors versus 2–5% who will book a sales call.

What it fixes

  • Framing — no more “why am I on a winery's website?”
  • Segmentation — a winery owner and a curious browser get different first screens
  • The dead end — the admin path can carry its own sign-in, so /admin never strands anyone

Watch out for

An interstitial is a door in front of the thing. Keep it to one screen, make “skip” genuinely prominent, and never show it twice to the same browser.

EffortLow· one new page, no changes to the product itself

Why it works

Your actual differentiator is that the guest-facing site and the back office are one system. Right now a visitor has to book, then remember to switch, then trust that what they're seeing is connected. Showing both panes at once removes the memory and the trust gap — the causal link is just visible.

None of the booking platforms researched — Bookeo, BookingPress, Cloudbeds, Toast — put both sides on screen simultaneously. It's the one thing here that nobody else is doing.

What it fixes

  • The core story, told in one screen instead of four steps
  • Shareability — this is the screenshot that goes on the landing page, in a pitch deck, in a WhatsApp message to a winery owner

Watch out for

Narrow screens can't hold two panes — on mobile it becomes a stacked "book, then watch it appear" with an auto-scroll. And the right pane needs to actually refresh when the left submits.

EffortHigh· a new two-pane route plus a refresh signal between them

Dimmed backdrop, ring on the real row, tooltip framed commercially rather than descriptively.

Why it works

The checklist that shipped today tells someone what to click. It never tells them why they should care. The spotlight pattern — dim, highlight, explain — is the standard walkthrough shape precisely because it controls attention: the visitor can't be looking at the wrong thing while you make the point.

The real upgrade here is the copy. “See it land in Orders” is a UI instruction. “A ₾600 booking that arrived at 23:40 while you slept” is a sales argument.

What it fixes

  • Nobody reads the corner box — a dimmed screen is unmissable
  • Feature → benefit, on every step
  • Gives you a place to name the money, which nothing on the demo currently does

Watch out for

Seven steps is the ceiling. Always-visible skip. And it must never dim a screen the visitor navigated to themselves — a tour that fights you is worse than no tour.

EffortMedium· mostly one component, replacing the checklist you already have

Packing sheets

Groups every open order into boxes and prints a sheet for the cellar. Most booking tools stop at the invoice.

The rail is the product's feature list, made clickable. Each item is a proof, not a claim.

Why it works

You've built a genuinely deep product and almost none of it is discoverable. A visitor would have to guess that packing sheets, per-company price tiers, masterclass add-ons, theme presets and a Georgian translation layer exist. The rail turns that invisible surface area into a menu — the “choose your own journey” pattern, which lets a prospect self-qualify on the feature they personally care about instead of sitting through your ordering.

What it fixes

  • Invisible depth — the strongest argument you have is currently unfindable
  • No forced sequence — a winery owner who only cares about wine logistics goes straight there
  • Doubles as the feature list for the marketing site, with each row deep-linking into live proof

Watch out for

A rail competes with the product's own navigation, and the admin already has a full nav row. It probably wants to be collapsible, or to live only on the public side.

EffortMedium· a rail component plus one annotation per destination screen

Before any of the four

Fill the demo with a winery that's actually trading

Every direction above still ends on No orders found unless the tenant has data. This is the highest-value work on the page and the least glamorous: roughly 18 months of plausible history — bookings behind and ahead, a revenue curve with a real summer peak, wine orders spread across pending, paid and delivered, a handful of tour companies on different price tiers.

It should regenerate on a schedule so it never drifts, and the “Finish setting up your account” banner must be suppressed for this tenant — a demo winery is not a winery mid-setup.
